## Key Question 1 — Kernel benchmarking: fair wall-clock and memory measurement of attention variants (FlashAttention-2/3, FlexAttention, linear attention, Mamba kernels)

### Takeaway
The de-facto standard protocol (FlashAttention-2/3, FlexAttention, Based, GLA) is: one GPU, fixed total tokens per call (batch × seqlen = 16k), a doubling sequence-length grid (512 → 16k), head dims 64/128, causal and non-causal, fp16/bf16, warm-up then many repetitions timed with CUDA events after synchronization, FLOPs counted as 4·B·L²·H·d (halved for causal, ×2.5 for backward), and GPU clocks fixed when possible. Linear-attention/SSD kernels do not beat FlashAttention-2 at short lengths: the published crossover is ≈1K–2K tokens on H100/A100 for GLA/SSD, while the FLA repository's own tables show FA2 still wins at T = 1024–2048 on a GB200; no primary source reports crossover numbers on RTX 3090/4090.

### Cited Findings

**FlashAttention-2 — protocol and FLOP accounting**
- Title: "FlashAttention-2: Faster Attention with Better Parallelism and Work Partitioning"; author Tri Dao; arXiv 2307.08691, July 2023 (venue not verified from fetched page). Kernel benchmarks on an A100 80GB SXM4; sequence lengths 512, 1k, … up to 16k; batch size set per length so that total tokens = 16k; hidden dim 2048 (32 heads at d=64, 16 heads at d=128); with and without causal mask. Forward FLOPs = 4 × seqlen² × head_dim × num_heads, halved for causal "because roughly half the entries are computed"; backward FLOPs = 2.5× forward (5 matmuls vs 2, including recomputation). Baselines: standard PyTorch attention, FlashAttention v1, xformers (CUTLASS) FlashAttention, Triton FlashAttention. — [ar5iv FA2 §4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.08691)
- Reported: ~2× over FA1/xformers; 1.3–1.5× (fwd) and ~2× (bwd) over Triton; up to ~10× over standard PyTorch; peak 230 TFLOPs/s (~73% of A100 peak, forward). H100 note: same code, no H100-specific features, reaches 335 TFLOPs/s, described as a lower bound. — [ar5iv FA2 §4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.08691)
- Named caveats: causal halving is approximate; end-to-end attention FLOPs are deliberately NOT halved (Megatron convention 6·L·params + 12·layers·hidden·L²), which understates causal utilization; block sizes hand-tuned per head dim. — [ar5iv FA2 §4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.08691)
- Official script `benchmarks/benchmark_flash_attention.py`: `repeats = 30` via `benchmark_fwd_bwd` from `flash_attn.utils.benchmark`; FLOP function `4*batch*seqlen²*nheads*headdim`, `/2` if causal, `×2.5` bwd, `×3.5` fwd+bwd; TFLOPs/s = FLOPs/time/1e12, NaN → 0; grid (batch, seqlen) ∈ {(32,512),(16,1024),(8,2048),(4,4096),(2,8192),(1,16384)}; headdim ∈ {64,128} with dim = 2048; causal ∈ {False, True}; fp16; dropout 0; baselines Flash2, naive PyTorch (NaN on failure), Triton (`sequence_parallel` on/off, faster bwd kept), xformers CUTLASS/Flash. — [GitHub benchmark script](https://github.com/Dao-AILab/flash-attention/blob/main/benchmarks/benchmark_flash_attention.py)

**FlashAttention-3 — protocol, variance control, numerical-error methodology**
- Title: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"; Jay Shah, Ganesh Bikshandi, Ying Zhang, Vijay Thakkar, Pradeep Ramani, Tri Dao; arXiv 2407.08608, July 2024 (venue not verified from fetched page). — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08608)
- Protocol: H100 80GB SXM5 (700 W); seqlen 512 → 16k; batch so total tokens = 16k; hidden 2048; head dim 64/128/256 (32/16/8 heads); causal and non-causal; FP16/BF16 fwd+bwd, FP8 fwd only; same FLOP formula as FA2 (causal halved; bwd 2.5×). Baselines: PyTorch standard, FA2 (CUDA), FA2 in Triton (H100 instructions), cuDNN FA2 (closed source). — [arXiv HTML FA3 §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08608v2)
- Variance control: "each benchmark is repeated 100 times and averaged"; GPU clock fixed at 1830 MHz "to reduce variability", which is also the clock used for the 989 TFLOPS theoretical peak; library versions pinned (CUDA 12.3, cuDNN 9.1.1.17, CUTLASS 3.5, PyTorch 2.3.0). — [arXiv HTML FA3 §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08608v2)
- Numerical error: RMSE vs an FP64 reference; inputs N(0,1) plus, for 0.1 % of entries, an extra N(0,100) term to simulate outlier features; FP16 FA2 = FA3 = 1.9e-4 vs baseline 3.2e-4; FP8 FA3 9.1e-3 vs baseline 2.4e-2 (2.6× lower). Caveat: synthetic outlier distribution, not real activations. — [arXiv HTML FA3 §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08608v2)
- Results: FP16 fwd 1.5–2.0× FA2, up to 740 TFLOPs/s (~75% util); bwd 1.5–1.75× FA2; ~1.5× over Triton FA2; FP8 close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s; ablation (non-causal FP16, batch 4, seqlen 8448, 16 heads, d=128): 661 → 582 (no warp specialization) → 570 (no GEMM-softmax pipelining) TFLOPs/s. — [arXiv HTML FA3 §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08608v2)

**FlexAttention — overheads of score_mod / block masks vs FA2**
- Title: "Flex Attention: A Programming Model for Generating Optimized Attention Kernels"; Juechu Dong, Boyuan Feng, Driss Guessous, Yanbo Liang, Horace He; arXiv 2412.05496, Dec 2024 (venue not verified from fetched page; MLSys 2025 publication is unverified). — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.05496)
- Hardware in paper: H100 power-capped at 650 W with memory bandwidth limited to 2.4 TB/s; A100 capped at 330 W; A6000 listed. Kernel benchmark: fixed KV size 256 MiB, head dim 64, bf16; causal training lengths 1k–64k (with/without GQA); variant comparisons at 16k-token QKV; decoding KV lengths 1k–132k. Timing method / warm-up / repeat count NOT stated in the paper text. Baselines: FAv2, FAv3 ("experimental"), FlashDecoding (FAKV), PyTorch SDPA with cuDNN 9.1.1 and memory-efficient backends. — [arXiv HTML FlexAttention](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05496v1)
- Numbers: causal training fwd 1.00–1.22× FAv2, bwd 0.86–1.05× FAv2; across 7 variants that FAv2 supports natively 0.68–1.43× FAv2; variants without native FAv2 support 5.49–8.00× vs SDPA with materialized masks; decoding 0.93–1.45× vs FAKV (one outlier GQA+ALiBi 5.37×, called a "software lottery" effect); paged attention <1 % runtime overhead; "full-block" optimization (skip masking on fully-dense blocks) ≈ 15 % improvement for causal-type masks; block-mask memory scales with ⌈Q_LEN/BS⌉×⌈KV_LEN/BS⌉, BS default 128. No standalone score_mod overhead number; converting mask_mod to score_mod "adds multiplication overhead". — [arXiv HTML FlexAttention](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05496v1)
- PyTorch blog (Aug 2024, updated 2025-05-30): on A100 vs FlashAttention-2 (script `benchmarks/transformer/score_mod.py`), FlexAttention reaches "90 % of FlashAttention2's performance in the forward pass and 85 % in the backward pass"; backward uses a deterministic algorithm that recomputes more intermediates. Applying a mask to every element (score_mod-style masking instead of mask_mod + BlockMask) costs "about a 15–20 % degradation in performance". `create_block_mask` is "relatively expensive" (hundreds of µs; use `_compile=True`, broadcast over B/H with None, or build BlockMask by hand) and must be cached; recompilation is NOT triggered when captured tensor values or block sparsity change, but BlockMask must be recomputed when sparsity changes. Numerics: not bitwise identical to FA, error distributions "nearly identical". Limitations at time of post: sequence lengths must be multiples of 128; FA3-level H100 performance not yet matched. — [PyTorch blog FlexAttention](https://pytorch.org/blog/flexattention/)
- Pitfall for structural-prior studies: a sliding-window causal mask is "significantly faster than FA2 with a causal mask as this mask has significantly more sparsity" — speedups from block masks are a function of block-level sparsity, so an unstructured or fine-grained graph mask that does not zero whole 128×128 blocks will not get this benefit. — [PyTorch blog FlexAttention](https://pytorch.org/blog/flexattention/)

**flash-linear-attention (FLA) — linear attention kernel practice and numbers**
- Tool: "FLA: A Triton-Based Library for Hardware-Efficient Implementations of Linear Attention Mechanism"; Songlin Yang, Yu Zhang; 2024 (GitHub). Benchmark command `python -m benchmarks.ops.run --op chunk_retention chunk_gla chunk_gdn flash_attn`; README tables "conducted on a single NVIDIA GB200 GPU (CUDA 12.9, PyTorch 2.9.0)", latencies in ms. — [fla-org/flash-linear-attention README](https://github.com/fla-org/flash-linear-attention)
- README forward latencies (Retention / GLA / GDN / FA2, ms): B=1,T=8192,H=96,D=128: 0.787 / 1.765 / 1.265 / 3.753; B=2,T=16384,H=16,D=128: 0.792 / 1.445 / 1.029 / 5.035; B=4,T=2048,H=16,D=128: 0.559 / 0.514 / 0.753 / 0.346; B=8,T=1024,H=8,D=64: 0.425 / 0.358 / 0.631 / 0.157. Fwd+bwd: B=1,T=8192,H=96,D=128: 2.618 / 7.670 / 4.738 / 15.371; B=2,T=16384: 2.122 / 5.984 / 3.616 / 19.960; B=8,T=2048,H=32,D=256: 51.103 / 13.797 / 8.644 / 6.748. i.e., FA2 is faster at T ≤ 2048 and linear kernels win at T ≥ 8192 on this hardware; one anomalous Retention entry (51.1 ms) is unexplained in the README. Varlen kernels supported since 2024-12; install requires a backend extra (`[cuda]`, `[rocm]`, …). — [fla-org/flash-linear-attention README](https://github.com/fla-org/flash-linear-attention)
- Gated Linear Attention paper (Songlin Yang, Bailin Wang, Yikang Shen, Rameswar Panda, Yoon Kim; arXiv 2312.06635, v6 Aug 2024; ICML 2024 not verified from fetched page): Fig. 2 benchmark on a single H100, batch 32, 16 heads, head dim 64, chunk size 64, log-scale length axes (~2^9–2^15); kernels: FlashLinearAttention with/without materialization, FlashAttention-2, pure PyTorch chunkwise. Abstract: FLA "faster than FlashAttention-2 … even on short sequence lengths (e.g., 1K)". Materializing chunk states costs ~10–20 % extra memory; recomputation is the default. Training throughput Fig. 6: 1.3B models on one H100, GLA advantage over Transformer++ grows beyond length 4096; Mamba lags both. Caveat stated by authors: most chunkwise implementations are "not I/O-aware and thus slower than FlashAttention for moderate sequence lengths (e.g., 2K-4K)"; single-GPU only; no larger-scale runs "due to limited compute". — [arXiv HTML GLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06635v6); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06635)

**Mamba / Mamba-2 (SSD) kernels**
- mamba-ssm README: benchmark script `benchmarks/benchmark_generation_mamba_simple.py` (latency at batch 1; throughput at `--batch 64`), no numbers in README; requires Linux, Python 3.10+, PyTorch 1.12+, CUDA 11.6+ for `selective_scan_cuda`; `pip install mamba-ssm --no-build-isolation`; `MAMBA_KEEP_CUDA_BUILD=TRUE` / `MAMBA_FORCE_BUILD=TRUE`; `causal-conv1d` extra; models trained with AMP (fp32 master weights) — frameworks storing fp16 params can destabilize SSMs; post-init hooks zeroing biases can override the Δ initialization. Citations: Gu & Dao, "Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces", arXiv 2312.00752, 2023; Dao & Gu, "Transformers are SSMs…", ICML 2024. — [state-spaces/mamba README](https://github.com/state-spaces/mamba)
- Mamba-2 paper (Tri Dao, Albert Gu; ICML 2024; arXiv 2405.21060): §9.3 compares SSD vs Mamba's fused scan vs FlashAttention-2; SSD is 2–8× faster than the fused scan (largest gains at state expansion N=64); "SSD is faster than FlashAttention-2 starting at sequence length 2K"; intro claims ~6× faster at 16K; at 4K the Mamba scan slows linearly with state size while SSD barely slows; the authors note the full Mamba-2 model "may be less efficient than a Transformer at short sequence lengths such as 2K". GPU/batch/head settings for Fig. 10 were not recoverable from the fetched text. — [arXiv HTML Mamba-2 (offset 100k)](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.21060v1); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21060)

**Lightning Attention-2 / MiniMax-01 — linear-attention vs FA2 at model level**

Caveat: LA1 is faster than LA2 at 1K–8K; text says A100 but a figure caption says A800; benchmarks are self-reported. — [ar5iv Lightning Attention-2](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.04658)
- MiniMax-01 (MiniMax, 89 authors, arXiv 2501.08313, Jan 2025): Fig. 8 training speed on a single-node H800 with a 3B model from 1,024 to 65,536 tokens, tokens/GPU/s; lightning is "the sole linear model that outperforms FlashAttention2"; 1B hybrid models: hybrid-lightning ≈33.4K TGS vs hybrid-hgrn2 29.5K vs hybrid-cosformer2 23.3K; at 1,024,000 tokens softmax attention is ~95 % of latency vs <12 % for lightning. Caveat: the original lightning implementation was "research-oriented and not ready for practical inference"; some gains are hardware-specific (H20 vs H800). — [arXiv HTML MiniMax-01](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.08313v1)

**Timing primitives and warm-up / synchronization guidance**
- `triton.testing.do_bench(fn, warmup=25, rep=100, grad_to_none=None, quantiles=None, return_mode='mean')`: warm-up and rep are in milliseconds; iteration counts are derived from a 5-call estimate (`n_warmup = max(1, warmup/estimate_ms)`, `n_repeat = max(1, rep/estimate_ms)`); each timed run clears an L2-sized cache buffer (`clear_cache`) and optionally resets `.grad`, then is bracketed by CUDA events; `quantiles` overrides `return_mode`. `do_bench_cudagraph` captures `n_repeat` unrolled calls in a CUDA graph, replays 10×, and does NOT flush L2 between runs. — [Triton do_bench docs](https://triton-lang.org/main/python-api/generated/triton.testing.do_bench.html); [triton/testing.py](https://github.com/triton-lang/triton/blob/main/python/triton/testing.py)
- PyTorch benchmark recipe: `timeit` "is not synchronizing CUDA and is thus only timing the time to launch the kernel"; `torch.utils.benchmark.Timer` synchronizes automatically; first CUDA call is slow (cuBLAS load) so warm-up is required and `blocked_autorange(min_run_time=0.2)` provides it; set `num_threads` explicitly; benchmark "a number of different inputs" because performance varies with shape/contiguity; validate that implementations produce the same output before timing; `Compare` tables; `Callgrind` instruction counts as a noise-free complement. — [PyTorch benchmark recipe](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/recipes/recipes/benchmark.html)

**Consumer GPUs (RTX 3090 / 4090)**
- flash-attention supports "Ampere, Ada, or Hopper GPUs (e.g., A100, RTX 3090, RTX 4090, H100)" in fp16/bf16; the search found no primary source reporting FA2 TFLOPs or linear-attention crossover lengths on RTX 3090/4090 (an FA1-era note says the 3090 showed 2.5–4.5× speedups over baseline because GDDR6X bandwidth is lower than A100 HBM). — [WebSearch summary citing flash-attention repo / Stanford CRFM FA2 post](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/07/17/flash2.html)

### Inferences
- A defensible single-GPU protocol for this thesis: (i) pin library versions and report them; (ii) fix GPU clocks if the driver allows (FA3 did 1830 MHz) or at least report power cap/thermal state; (iii) grid = FA2 grid (batch×seqlen = 16k; 512→16k doubling; d ∈ {64,128}; causal/non-causal; bf16) plus the graph-relevant regime (short sequences, 128–2048 tokens, where FLA/SSD are slower than FA2 according to the FLA README and GLA/Mamba-2 papers); (iv) time with `do_bench` (L2 flush, CUDA events, ≥100 ms warm-up, report median and 20/80 quantiles) and cross-check one point with `torch.utils.benchmark.Timer`; (v) report FLOPs with the FA2 formula and state explicitly whether causal halving is applied; (vi) report peak memory via `torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated` after `reset_peak_memory_stats` (standard, but not from a fetched source); (vii) for FlexAttention report block-sparsity of the mask, BlockMask construction cost and whether it was cached, and whether `torch.compile` was warm; (viii) for Mamba/FLA kernels report state size N and chunk size since speed depends on them (Mamba-2 §9.3, GLA §3.3).
- Because FlexAttention costs 10–15 % vs FA2 even with no mask and partial-block masking costs ~15–20 %, a "structural prior implemented as a mask" should be compared against FA2-dense at equal sequence length AND against FlexAttention-dense (noop mask) so kernel overhead is not confused with prior cost.
- Crossover claims differ by implementation generation and hardware (GLA: ≤1K on H100 in 2023 paper; FLA README on GB200 in 2025: FA2 still ahead at 2K); the thesis should measure its own crossover rather than cite one, and cite the FLA README as evidence that it moves.

### Gaps
- No primary source found reporting linear-attention vs FlashAttention crossover lengths on RTX 3090/4090; consumer-GPU numbers must be measured.
- FlexAttention paper does not state timing method, warm-up or repeat counts; the blog's "90 %/85 % of FA2" figure is A100-only.
- Mamba-2 §9.3 hardware/batch/head configuration was not recoverable from the fetched HTML chunks (the text was cut mid-section).
- Exact ms/GB values for GLA Fig. 2 and Lightning Attention-2 Fig. 3 are only in figures, not text.
- Whether do_bench's L2 flush buffer is 256 MB was not confirmed from the fetched source (it calls `get_empty_cache_for_benchmark()` / `clear_cache`).

## Key Question 2 — Pareto / compute-matched reporting in efficiency papers

### Takeaway
Respected efficiency papers report quality against at least two cost axes (recurrent state size or memory, and measured throughput/latency on a named GPU), match models by parameters AND training tokens with identical data order, and sweep learning rates per architecture; Griffin, Mamba-2 and MiniMax-01 additionally report Chinchilla-style loss-vs-FLOPs curves and separately wall-clock. The field's own critiques (LRA desiderata, Amos et al., Efficient Transformers survey, GraphGPS/Tönshoff) name the key pitfalls: big-O ≠ wall-clock, implementation maturity bias, single-scale rankings that flip with scale, and under-tuned baselines.

### Cited Findings

**Recall–memory / recall–throughput Pareto (Based, Zoology)**
- Based Fig. 2: x-axis = recurrent state size in bytes during generation; y-axis = MQAR accuracy; each architecture trained at several hyperparameter settings that change state size (model dim, feature dim, window). MQAR train: length-256 sequences with 4–64 KV pairs; eval: length 1,024 with 4–256 pairs. Parameter/token matching: 360M and 1.3B scales; 10B/30B/50B Pile tokens; "each model sees the same tokens … in the same order"; matching is approximate (Based 1.35B vs Transformer++ 1.33B vs Mamba 1.32B). Seeds/repeat counts not stated. — [arXiv HTML Based](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.18668v2)
- Zoology (Simran Arora, Sabri Eyuboglu, Aman Timalsina, Isys Johnson, Michael Poli, James Zou, Atri Rudra, Christopher Ré; arXiv 2312.04927, Dec 2023; ICLR 2024 not verified from fetched page): 17 models, scales 70M–1.4B, same Pile data/order/tokenizer and GPT-NeoX infra on A100 80GB; LLaMA-style backbone with only the sequence mixer varied; MQAR vocab 8,192, sequence lengths 64–512, two-layer backbones; four learning rates per architecture spanning 1e-4 to 1e-2 (log grid), reporting the max test accuracy across the sweep; Fig. 2 plots model dimension vs MQAR accuracy by sequence length. Pitfalls named: average perplexity hides gaps (they slice tokens into AR-hits vs others); single-query synthetics failed to reproduce real-data gaps, multi-query was needed. — [ar5iv Zoology](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.04927)
- Mamba-2 §9.1 uses "a harder version" of MQAR with T ∈ {256, 512, 1024}, T/4 KV pairs, vocab 8192, curriculum over T/32…T/4 pairs with ~2^18 examples each, smaller models than usual; compares controlled state sizes N ∈ {16, 64, 256}. — [arXiv HTML Mamba-2 (offset 200k)](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.21060v1)

**FLOP-matched scaling curves plus wall-clock (Griffin, Mamba-2, MiniMax-01)**
- Griffin (Soham De et al., 17 authors; arXiv 2402.19427, Feb 2024): three families (MQA Transformer, Hawk, Griffin) from 100M to 7B (+14B Griffin); tokens scale with parameters per Chinchilla; MassiveText, 2048-token sequences; axes = validation loss vs training FLOPs, both log; training speed on TPU-v3 with total tokens per batch fixed while sequence length varies, step time reported RELATIVE to the MQA baseline at 2K; inference latency at batch 16 with empty vs 4096-token prefill; throughput = max tokens/s on one device. Caveats stated: RG-LRU scan is memory-bound (FLOPs/byte ≈ 0.75 vs device ≈ 4.2), a custom Pallas kernel is ~3× faster than native JAX scan; associative scan reduces FLOPs but was up to 50 % slower; Griffin trains slightly slower than MQA at 7B/short sequences despite fewer FLOPs; MQA baseline has slightly fewer parameters. — [ar5iv Griffin](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.19427)
- Mamba-2 §9.2: Pile; GPT-3 model specs 125M–2.7B (scaling plot ~125M–1.3B); Chinchilla protocol; baselines Mamba and Transformer++ (RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm, no biases, higher LR); Fig. 9 claims Pareto dominance on perplexity vs theoretical FLOPs AND vs wall-clock. — [arXiv HTML Mamba-2 (offset 100k)](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.21060v1)
- MiniMax-01 scaling study: 70M–7B, up to 300B tokens at 8192 context, 4M-token batches, fixed LR 3e-4, WD 0.1; Chinchilla-style power-law fits L = a·C^b (softmax 3.7087·C^-0.0798; lightning 3.5391·C^-0.0768; hybrid 3.4797·C^-0.0763); FLOPs counted per Kaplan; training loss used as proxy for test loss; plus downstream CSR, NIAH, SCROLLS. Caveat: pure lightning has limited retrieval (NIAH), so they adopt 1 softmax per 7 lightning layers. — [arXiv HTML MiniMax-01](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.08313v1)

**Benchmarks and their critiques (LRA, Amos et al., Efficient Transformers survey, Tay et al. 2022)**
- Long Range Arena (Yi Tay, Mostafa Dehghani, Samira Abnar, Yikang Shen, Dara Bahri, Philip Pham, Jinfeng Rao, Liu Yang, Sebastian Ruder, Donald Metzler; arXiv 2011.04006, Nov 2020; ICLR 2021 not verified from fetched page): tasks ListOps 2K, Text 4K, Retrieval 4K (8K pair), Image 1K, Pathfinder 1K, Path-X 16K; efficiency measured on byte-level text at 1K/2K/3K/4K, batch 32, 4×4 TPU V3, metrics = steps/s (speedup vs vanilla) and per-device peak memory (GB); Fig. 3 = score vs speed with memory as bubble size. Fairness rules: fixed layers/heads/embedding sizes for all models; no pretraining; task setup unaltered; authors state "it is non-trivial and almost impossible to conduct a perfectly fair evaluation of all models". — [ar5iv LRA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.04006)
- "Never Train from Scratch: Fair Comparison of Long-Sequence Models Requires Data-Driven Priors" (Ido Amos, Jonathan Berant, Ankit Gupta; arXiv 2310.02980, v4 Apr 2024; ICLR 2024 not verified from fetched page): random initialization "leads to overestimating architecture differences"; denoising pretraining on the task data alone makes vanilla Transformers match S4 on LRA and improves PathX-256 by 20 absolute points; structured parameterizations become "mostly redundant" with data-driven initialization; recommendation: pretraining-based evaluation "is essential for reliable performance estimation". — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02980)
- "Efficient Transformers: A Survey" (Yi Tay, Mostafa Dehghani, Dara Bahri, Donald Metzler; arXiv 2009.06732, v3 Mar 2022; ACM CSUR not verified from fetched page): "there is not an easy way to compare these models side by side"; differing model sizes/configs make it "difficult to correctly attribute the reason for the performance gains"; "complexity does not always translate to real world throughput or latency"; linear-complexity models can be slower than quadratic ones; many linear attention models show no speed/memory gain at short sequences; "efficient" is often misread as "fast". — [ar5iv Efficient Transformers survey](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2009.06732)
- "Scaling Laws vs Model Architectures: How does Inductive Bias Influence Scaling?" (Yi Tay, Mostafa Dehghani, Samira Abnar, Hyung Won Chung, William Fedus, Jinfeng Rao, Sharan Narang, Vinh Q. Tran, Dani Yogatama, Donald Metzler; arXiv 2207.10551, 2022): ten architectures; "the best performing model can fluctuate at different scales"; scaling behavior differs across inductive biases upstream and downstream; implication: single-scale comparisons are misleading. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10551)

**Graph-transformer efficiency reporting (GraphGPS, Exphormer, LRGB re-evaluation)**
- GraphGPS (Ladislav Rampášek, Mikhail Galkin, Vijay Prakash Dwivedi, Anh Tuan Luu, Guy Wolf, Dominique Beaini; arXiv 2205.12454; NeurIPS 2022 not verified from fetched page): parameter budgets ~500k (ZINC, PATTERN, CLUSTER, LRGB) and ~100k (MNIST, CIFAR10); 10 seeds on main benchmarks, 4 on LRGB, single run on PCQM4Mv2, 3–4 seeds in ablations; mean ± std. Linear attention: Performer/BigBird underperform full Transformer (CIFAR10: 72.31 Transformer / 70.67 Performer / 70.48 BigBird / 69.95 none) and are NOT faster on small graphs (ZINC 18 s/epoch Performer vs 14 s Transformer; CIFAR10 77 s vs 62 s); MalNet-Tiny (≤5k nodes): Transformer roughly doubles runtime vs Performer; molpcba 196 s/epoch vs 883 s for SAN. Caveats: hyperparameter sensitivity; lack of datasets requiring long range where linear attention's scalability would matter. — [ar5iv GraphGPS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.12454)
- Exphormer (Hamed Shirzad, Ameya Velingker, Balaji Venkatachalam, Danica J. Sutherland, Ali Kemal Sinop; arXiv 2303.06147; ICML 2023 not verified from fetched page): built inside GraphGPS, budgets matched (~500K PATTERN/CLUSTER, ~100K CIFAR10/MNIST); expander degree 6–22 and virtual nodes 1–6 chosen by linear (not grid) search; number of runs behind ± NOT stated; memory reported qualitatively (OOM / max batch size on a 40GB A100: dense GPS OOM at batch 16 on MalNet-Tiny vs Exphormer at 256; dense transformer does not fit ogbn-arxiv even at 2 layers/32 hidden); wall-clock mixed (CIFAR10 115 s/epoch Exphormer vs 62 s dense GPS; PATTERN 21 vs 18 s) — "the main advantage is memory, not speed"; BigBird/Performer on ogbn-arxiv are slower and less accurate than a GCN-only baseline; some baseline numbers copied from prior papers. — [arXiv HTML Exphormer](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.06147v2)
- Tönshoff et al. (see KQ4) show the GT-vs-MPNN gap on LRGB vanished after tuning dropout/depth/LR/head depth within the 500k budget — a direct warning that structural-prior claims can be artefacts of baseline tuning. — [arXiv HTML Tönshoff](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.00367v2)

### Inferences
- Matching rule hierarchy used by strong papers: (1) same data, same order, same tokenizer/infra; (2) parameter-matched within ~2 %; (3) token-matched; (4) report FLOPs (Kaplan/Chinchilla accounting) AND measured wall-clock/memory on a named GPU; (5) per-architecture learning-rate sweep (Zoology: 4 LRs log-spaced, report best). For a thesis the minimal credible set is (1)–(3) plus one LR sweep and both cost axes.
- Recommended plots: (a) quality vs log(state size or peak memory) with one curve per architecture family (Based style); (b) quality vs training FLOPs AND the same quality vs wall-clock (Mamba-2/Griffin style; the divergence between the two is itself a finding); (c) LRA-style score-vs-speed with memory as marker size for a compact summary.
- Implementation-maturity bias must be declared: FA2/FA3 are CUDA/CUTLASS kernels tuned for years; FlexAttention/FLA/SSD are Triton. Report a "same-backend" comparison (e.g., FlexAttention-dense vs FlexAttention-masked) alongside the cross-backend one.
- Because rankings flip with scale (Tay 2022) and with pretraining (Amos 2024), the protocol should pre-register the scale(s) and whether any pretraining/denoising warm-start is used, and avoid generalizing beyond them.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gives a formal definition of "wall-clock-matched" comparisons (equal training time budgets); Griffin reports relative step time and Mamba-2 plots loss vs wall-clock, but neither trains to a fixed wall-clock budget.
- Seeds/repeats for Based and Exphormer runs are not stated in their texts.

## Key Question 3 — Learning-curve / data-efficiency methodology

### Takeaway
Learning curves should be built on a geometric (doubling) grid of training-set sizes, with hyperparameters re-tuned per size, repeated over random draws, plotted log-log, and summarized by a fitted power law with offset (a·n^-b + c) whose fit is validated on held-out curve points; AUC-style summaries are explicitly warned against because they hide crossings. Scaling-law papers give concrete recipes (Hestness: 2× shards + per-shard model search; Kaplan: L(N,D) and L(N,S) forms; Chinchilla: three fitting approaches, Huber-loss parametric fit, LR schedule matched to the token horizon).

### Cited Findings
- "The Shape of Learning Curves: a Review" (Tom Viering, Marco Loog; arXiv 2103.10948, v2 Nov 2022; TPAMI not verified from fetched page). Learning curve = expected risk vs training-set size n (vs "training curve" = performance vs optimization steps; deep-learning papers conflate them). Parametric catalogue: POW3 a·n^-b + c (often best because the offset models nonzero asymptotic error), POW2, LOG2, EXP3/EXP2, LIN2, VAP3, MMF4, WBL4, EXP4, EXPP3, POW4, ILOG2, EXPD3. Practice: shrink training folds to target sizes inside k-fold CV or draw random subsets, repeat and average; stratify; use a logarithmic grid of sizes; show std over folds as error bars; "hyperparameters should ideally be tuned separately for each training size". Extrapolation: fit, then estimate samples needed for a target; validate fits on held-out curve points; progressive sampling with a geometric series. Ill-behaved curves: phase transitions, peaking/double descent (n ≈ d), dipping, non-monotone ERM, crossing curves. Pitfalls: "Summarizing with the area under the learning curve treats all sample sizes as equally likely and can hide crossings"; least squares on log-values ≠ minimizing error in original space; models without an offset fit bounded metrics poorly; many deep-learning claims rest on visual inspection without competing models; reusing hyperparameters across sizes "produces suboptimal curves". v2 adds scepticism that power laws are proven accurate in many cases. — [ar5iv Viering & Loog](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.10948); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10948)
- "Deep Learning Scaling is Predictable, Empirically" (Joel Hestness, Sharan Narang, Newsha Ardalani, Gregory Diamos, Heewoo Jun, Hassan Kianinejad, Md. Mostofa Ali Patwary, Yang Yang, Yanqi Zhou; arXiv 1712.00409, Dec 2017). Method: shuffle data, split into shards spanning 2–3 orders of magnitude with each step ~2× larger; one fixed validation set; on the smallest shard grid-search model size with regularization removed; for each larger shard search sizes projected from the previous best; also search batch size, LR, sometimes seeds; fit ε(m) = α·m^β_g + γ. Exponents: NMT −0.128 (composite), word LM −0.066, char LM −0.094, ResNet top-1 −0.309 (top-5 −0.488; metric-dependent), speech ≈ −0.30. Three regions: small-data (≈ random guessing; ImageNet below ~25 images/class), power-law, irreducible error. Caveats: larger models/shards harder to optimize, error drifts above trend until batch/LR retuned; departures from power law were used as cues to retune; ~50 GPU-years of compute. — [ar5iv Hestness](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.00409)
- "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, Tom Henighan, Tom B. Brown, Benjamin Chess, Rewon Child, Scott Gray, Alec Radford, Jeffrey Wu, Dario Amodei; arXiv 2001.08361, Jan 2020). L(N) = (Nc/N)^0.076, Nc ≈ 8.8e13; L(D) = (Dc/D)^0.095, Dc ≈ 5.4e13; L(Cmin) exponent 0.050; joint L(N,D) = [(Nc/N)^(αN/αD) + Dc/D]^αD; learning curve L(N,S) = (Nc/N)^αN + (Sc/Smin)^αS with αS ≈ 0.76; overfitting bound D ≳ 5e3·N^0.74; larger models are more sample-efficient (~100× from smallest to largest). Caveats: no theoretical basis; fits valid only in the power-law region; small-data regime poorly fit; regularization/augmentation unexplored; constants shift between sections (e.g., αD 0.095 vs 0.103). — [ar5iv Kaplan](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.08361)
- "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (Jordan Hoffmann, Sebastian Borgeaud, Arthur Mensch + 19; arXiv 2203.15556, Mar 2022; NeurIPS 2022 not verified from fetched page). >400 models, 70M–16B params, 5B–500B tokens. Approach 1: fix sizes, vary tokens with four cosine cycle lengths, take the lower envelope of loss per FLOP (a = b = 0.50). Approach 2: nine IsoFLOP budgets, vary size, fit a parabola per budget (a = 0.49, b = 0.51). Approach 3: L(N,D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β, fit by Huber loss (δ = 1e-3) on log loss with L-BFGS from a grid of inits: E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28 (a = 0.46, b = 0.54). Caveat: cosine cycle must match the token horizon; overshooting by >25 % degrades final loss. Conclusion: scale N and D equally (~20 tokens/param implied by 70B/1.4T). — [ar5iv Hoffmann](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.15556); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)
- Dodge et al.'s expected-validation-performance-vs-budget curve (see KQ4) is the standard way to express tuning-budget efficiency as a curve rather than a point. — [ar5iv Dodge](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.03004)

### Inferences
- Fraction grid: a geometric grid (e.g., 1/64, 1/32, …, 1/2, 1 of the training set, or shards doubling per Hestness) is the only grid endorsed by the fetched sources; linear grids are explicitly discouraged (Viering & Loog).
- Seeds: no fetched learning-curve source prescribes a count; Viering & Loog require repetition over random draws with variance shown, Hestness sometimes searched seeds; combined with Bouthillier (KQ4) the data split should be re-drawn per repetition, not only the init seed.
- Summaries to report: (a) fitted POW3 parameters with fit quality and held-out validation of the fit; (b) "data needed to reach X % of full-data accuracy" read off the fitted curve, with a bootstrap interval; (c) if AUC is reported, pair it with the full curves because AUC hides crossings.
- Per-size hyperparameter re-tuning is expensive; the compromise supported by Hestness is to tune at the smallest size and project forward, re-tuning when the curve departs from the power law.

### Gaps
- No source fetched gives a standard "data to reach X % of full-data accuracy" protocol by name; this is an inference from the extrapolation practice in Viering & Loog.
- Recommended seed counts for learning curves are not stated in any fetched source.

## Key Question 4 — Statistics, multi-seed practice, pre-registration, and graph-benchmark reproducibility

### Takeaway
Randomize data splits as well as initialization (data sampling is the largest variance source), report interval estimates (percentile/stratified bootstrap) rather than point estimates, use paired non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon for two systems over many datasets; Friedman + post-hoc for several; paired bootstrap/permutation over test items for single-dataset metrics), publish the hyperparameter budget and expected-validation-performance curves, and pre-register hypotheses and analysis before confirmatory runs. Graph benchmarks have repeatedly shown rankings flip with splits, tuning budget, and metric implementation.

### Cited Findings

**Variance and seeds**
- "Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks" (Xavier Bouthillier + 16 co-authors; MLSys 2021 per submission note; arXiv 2103.03098). Sources: data sampling (splits/bootstrap), augmentation, weight init, dropout, data order; HPO choices. "Bootstrapping data stands out as the most important source of variance"; weight init generally <50 % of bootstrap variance, on par with data order; HPO variance "not negligible", about as large as init. Recommendations: "As many sources of variation as possible should be randomized whenever possible"; use multiple data splits / out-of-bootstrap resampling instead of a fixed test set; decide with the probability of outperforming P(A>B) over paired splits, percentile-bootstrap CI, significant if lower CI bound > 0.5, meaningful if upper bound exceeds γ = 0.75. Estimators: IdealEst re-runs HPO per sample, O(k·T) (≈1,070 h at k=100); FixHOptEst(k, All) randomizes everything except HPO, O(k+T) (≈21 h), "51× reduction in compute cost" and close to ideal. Error rates in simulation: single-run comparison ~10 % false positives / ~75 % false negatives; average comparison ~90 % false negatives; the recommended test ~5 % / ~30 %. Pitfall: fixing seeds correlates runs and can increase estimator variance. No single recommended seed count. — [ar5iv Bouthillier](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03098); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098)
- "Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters" (Peter Henderson, Riashat Islam, Philip Bachman, Joelle Pineau, Doina Precup, David Meger; AAAI 2018; arXiv 1709.06560). Two disjoint sets of 5 seeds of the same TRPO configuration on HalfCheetah-v1 differed with t = −9.09, p = 0.0016 ("statistically different distributions can be attained just from the random seeds"); bootstrap CIs with 10k iterations (pivotal); t-test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov and bootstrap percent-difference reported; top-N seed reporting called misleading; different codebases of the same algorithm give different results; report all hyperparameters and package code; power analysis suggested but "an open problem"; max return alone "typically inadequate". — [ar5iv Henderson](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)
- "Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice" (Rishabh Agarwal, Max Schwarzer, Pablo Samuel Castro, Aaron Courville, Marc G. Bellemare; NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper; arXiv 2108.13264). Recommendations: stratified bootstrap CIs (resample runs per task), performance profiles with pointwise bands, interquartile mean (IQM), optimality gap, average probability of improvement; prefer CIs over p-value thresholds. Typical practice 3–10 runs; median CIs would need ~50–100 runs; percentile-bootstrap CIs for IQM behave well from ~10 runs; with 3 runs bootstrap CIs under-cover. Atari-100k case study with 100 runs/algorithm: rankings shifted under subsampling; max-during-training evaluation protocols inflated scores. Library: rliable. — [ar5iv Agarwal](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264); [rliable](https://agarwl.github.io/rliable)

**Budget-aware reporting**
- "Show Your Work: Improved Reporting of Experimental Results" (Jesse Dodge, Suchin Gururangan, Dallas Card, Roy Schwartz, Noah A. Smith; arXiv 1909.03004, 2019; EMNLP venue not verified from fetched page). Estimator: Ê[V*_n | n] = Σ_v v·(P̂(V ≤ v)^n − P̂(V < v)^n) with the empirical CDF of validation scores from random search — no extra training needed; plot vs budget n (trials or time) with ±1 std; rankings can change with budget (logistic regression beats CNN on SST up to 10 trials; GloVe vs ELMo fine-tuning flips with time budget). Checklist: computing infrastructure, average runtime per approach, train/val/test details, validation performance for each reported test result, code link; for HP search: bounds per HP, best configs, number of trials, selection method/criterion, expected validation performance curve. — [ar5iv Dodge](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.03004)

**Significance testing and multiple comparisons**
- "Statistical Comparisons of Classifiers over Multiple Data Sets" (Janez Demšar; JMLR 7:1–30, 2006). Recommends Wilcoxon signed-ranks for two classifiers over N datasets and the Friedman test with post-hoc tests for several, presented with critical-difference (CD) diagrams; argues the paired t-test is "conceptually inappropriate and statistically unsafe" here for three reasons: commensurability of scores across datasets, normality needed with small samples (~30 datasets) that cannot be checked, sensitivity to outliers; averaging accuracies across datasets is "meaningless" if datasets are not commensurable; surveys ICML papers finding t-tests dominant and Bonferroni used in few. — [JMLR PDF](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume7/demsar06a/demsar06a.pdf); [JMLR page](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v7/demsar06a.html)
- "The Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in NLP" (Rotem Dror, Gili Baumer, Segev Shlomov, Roi Reichart; ACL 2018, pp. 1383–1392). Decision procedure: if the test-statistic distribution is known/normal → parametric (paired Student's t-test; check with Shapiro–Wilk / Kolmogorov–Smirnov); otherwise sampling-free non-parametric (sign test, McNemar for paired binary classification, Cochran's Q, Wilcoxon signed-rank — "more powerful than the sign test") or sampling-based (Pitman permutation, paired bootstrap; preferred when the measure is not an average over items, e.g., F1/BLEU). Survey of ACL/TACL 2017: significance testing "often ignored or misused"; misuse cases include t-test on non-average measures. Open issue flagged: a "significance framework for multiple comparisons" across datasets. — [ACL Anthology P18-1128](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)

**Pre-registration**
- Pre-registration workshop (ICCV 2019, NeurIPS 2020, NeurIPS 2021, 13 Dec 2021; organizers Samuel Albanie, João F. Henriques, Luca Bertinetto, Alex Hernández-García, Hazel Doughty, Gül Varol). Two-stage model: a ≤5-page proposal without confirmatory experiments is reviewed and published non-archivally; after acceptance authors run the experiments and submit proposal+results to a PMLR volume, reviewed for "a good-faith attempt to complete the experiments described in their proposal". Rationale: publication bias toward benchmark gains, publishable negative results, and separating hypothesis generation from confirmation to raise statistical significance. 2020 full papers: PMLR v148. — [preregister.science](https://preregister.science/); [PMLR v148](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v148/)

**Graph-benchmark reproducibility**
- "A Fair Comparison of Graph Neural Networks for Graph Classification" (Federico Errica, Marco Podda, Davide Bacciu, Alessio Micheli; ICLR 2020; arXiv 1912.09893). Protocol: 10-fold stratified CV for risk assessment; per outer fold an inner 90/10 holdout for model selection; after selection train 3 times on the outer training fold (10 % held out for early stopping) and average test scores; grids of 32–72 configs per model with similar parameter upper bounds; precomputed identical splits; >47,000 runs. Pitfalls found in prior work: reporting validation accuracy of 10-fold CV as test (GIN), tuning on one fold and reusing (DGCNN), unstratified/unseeded splits (DiffPool), missing selection code/splits, missing std. Validation accuracy was always ≥ test. Structure-agnostic baseline best on D&D, PROTEINS, ENZYMES; GNNs clearly better on NCI1; adding degree features makes the baseline competitive on social datasets. — [ar5iv Errica](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.09893)
- "Pitfalls of Graph Neural Network Evaluation" (Oleksandr Shchur, Maximilian Mumme, Aleksandar Bojchevski, Stephan Günnemann; arXiv 1811.05868, 2018, v2 2019; workshop venue not verified from fetched page). Protocol: 100 random splits × 20 random initializations; 20 labels/class train, 30/class val, rest test; identical optimizer (Adam), early stopping and grid (hidden size, LR, dropout, L2) for all models; mean ± std (std "only a rough guide" because scores are non-normal). Findings: on the Planetoid split GAT ranks first on CORA/CiteSeer and GCN on PubMed, but on another split of the same size GCN leads CORA/CiteSeer and MoNet PubMed — "how fragile and misleading results obtained on a single split can be"; GAT has very high variance on Amazon datasets (some inits <40 %). — [ar5iv Shchur](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.05868)
- "Where Did the Gap Go? Reassessing the Long-Range Graph Benchmark" (Jan Tönshoff, Martin Ritzert, Eran Rosenbluth, Martin Grohe; arXiv 2309.00367, Sept 2023; LoG 2023 venue not verified from fetched page). Within the 500k budget they tuned dropout {0, 0.1, 0.2}, depth {6, 8, 10}, LR {1e-3, 5e-4, 1e-4}, head depth {1, 2, 3}, PE/SE {none, LapPE, RWSE}; one-at-a-time search, one run per config, final results averaged over 4 seeds. Before → after: Peptides-func AP GCN 0.5930 → 0.6860, GINE 0.5498 → 0.6621, GatedGCN 0.6069 → 0.6765, GPS 0.6535 → 0.6534; Peptides-struct MAE GCN 0.3496 → 0.2460 (SOTA), GPS 0.2500 → 0.2509; PascalVOC-SP F1 GCN 0.1268 → 0.2078, GINE 0.1265 → 0.2718, GatedGCN 0.2873 → 0.3880, GPS 0.3748 → 0.4440; COCO-SP F1 GPS 0.3412 → 0.3884. A 2-layer prediction head explains almost all of the Peptides-struct gap. Feature normalization (channel-wise, training-split stats) was missing in all LRGB works except CRaWl and accounts for ≥ half of gains on vision datasets. PCQM-Contact: code computed raw MRR though filtered MRR was specified; removing self-loops raised MRR ~10 points and re-ordered models. Recommendations: tune baselines as carefully as the proposed method; normalize features by default; implement metrics exactly as specified and state negative filtering; test whether long-range advantages hold beyond superpixel tasks. — [arXiv HTML Tönshoff](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.00367v2)
- Platonov et al. heterophily protocol (see KQ5): 10 random 50/25/25 splits, one run per split, mean ± std; accuracy for multiclass, ROC-AUC for binary; squirrel/chameleon duplicates (2,978 of 5,201 and 1,387 of 2,277 nodes) cause train–test leakage and re-rank models (FSGNN 1st → 10th on squirrel). — [arXiv HTML Platonov](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.11640v2)

### Inferences
- A protocol consistent with all fetched sources: ≥10 paired repetitions where each repetition redraws the data split AND the init seed; same HPO budget per architecture (report Dodge curves); aggregate with IQM or mean plus percentile/stratified bootstrap CIs; decision rule = Bouthillier's P(A>B) with γ = 0.75, or Wilcoxon signed-rank across datasets/repetitions with Holm correction when several variants are compared against one control (Demšar names Bonferroni-Dunn/Holm-type post-hoc procedures for control comparisons; details beyond the abstract were only partially extracted); for single-dataset per-item metrics use paired bootstrap over test items (Dror).
- Henderson's 5-vs-5 seed experiment and Agarwal's 3-run under-coverage together argue that 3–5 seeds are insufficient for claims of small differences; 10 is the smallest count any fetched source treats as adequate for bootstrap CIs on IQM.
- A pre-registration document for the thesis should fix: hypotheses, architectures and budgets, split/seed scheme, primary metric, decision rule and γ, and the Pareto axes, before confirmatory runs, following the proposal-then-results format of the workshop.

### Gaps
- Multiple-comparison correction in ML beyond Demšar (e.g., Holm vs Bonferroni-Dunn guidance) was only visible as keyword hits in the PDF; the exact procedure text was not extracted.
- No single fetched source prescribes a minimum seed count for deep-learning benchmarks; Bouthillier explicitly declines to.

## Key Question 5 — Dataset practice: synthetic graph-reasoning generators, text-attributed graphs with raw text, code datasets with exact labels, and label noise in vulnerability data

### Takeaway
Several generators give controllable size/difficulty with labels computed by exact algorithms (NetworkX / reference implementations): GraphQA (ER/BA/SFN/SBM/star/path/complete, 5–20 nodes), NLGraph (n, p per difficulty tier, 5–35 nodes), GraphArena (real subgraphs by random-walk sampling, exact solvers, 4–50 nodes), GraphWiz/GraphInstruct (ER, ≤100 nodes), Luo et al. GraphInstruct (ER/BA/small-world, 5–35 nodes, 21 tasks), CLRS-30 (algorithm traces, 16-node train / 64-node test). Raw text is available for Cora/PubMed/ogbn-arxiv/arxiv-2023 (TAPE) and the CS-TAG datasets; Platonov's roman-empire/amazon-ratings ship fastText features, not raw text. POJ-104 and CodeNet give exact functional labels by problem ID, whereas vulnerability datasets have documented label accuracy of 25–60 % and up to 18.9 % train–test duplication, justifying exclusion.

### Cited Findings

**Synthetic graph-reasoning generators**
- GraphQA / "Talk like a Graph: Encoding Graphs for Large Language Models" (Bahare Fatemi, Jonathan Halcrow, Bryan Perozzi; arXiv 2310.04560, Oct 2023; ICLR 2024 not verified from fetched page). Generators via NetworkX: Erdős–Rényi (p ~ U[0,1]), scale-free, Barabási–Albert, SBM (2–10 communities), plus star, path, complete; 5–20 nodes; 500 graphs each for ER/BA/SFN/SBM and 100 each for path/complete/star; seven tasks in the paper (edge existence, node degree, node count, edge count, connected nodes, cycle check, disconnected nodes); answers computed by NetworkX; code/data promised "upon acceptance". — [arXiv HTML GraphQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04560v1)
- NLGraph / "Can Language Models Solve Graph Problems in Natural Language?" (Heng Wang, Shangbin Feng, Tianxing He, Zhaoxuan Tan, Xiaochuang Han, Yulia Tsvetkov; NeurIPS 2023 Spotlight; arXiv 2305.10037). Eight tasks: connectivity, cycle, topological sort, shortest path, maximum flow, bipartite matching, Hamilton path, GNN simulation. Generator controlled by node count n and edge probability p per tier: connectivity easy 5–10 / medium 11–25 / hard 26–35 nodes with p ∈ {0.3, 0.7, 1.0} (hard {0.3, 0.7}); shortest path easy 5–10 (p ∈ {0.5,0.7,0.9}, w 1–4, ℓ 2–6), hard 11–20 (p ∈ {0.2,0.25}, w 1–10); max flow easy 5–10 (p ∈ {0.2,0.3}, c 1–10), hard 11–20 (p 0.25, c 1–20); etc. Standard set 5,902 problems; extended set 29,370. Partial credit: shortest path 1/rank; max flow t/s. Pitfalls named: spurious correlations (connectivity accuracy 74.67 general vs 51.67 on chains); accuracy falls with path length; wording changes ("city/road") shift results; "the generator is a simple edge-probability model". Code: github.com/Arthur-Heng/NLGraph. — [ar5iv NLGraph](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.10037); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10037)
- GraphArena (Jianheng Tang, Qifan Zhang, Yuhan Li, Nuo Chen, Jia Li; ICLR 2025; arXiv 2407.00379). Ten tasks, 500 small + 500 large graphs each (10,000 problems): Common Neighbor (DBLP, 4–19 / 20–50 nodes), Shortest Distance (DBpedia, 4–19 / 20–50), Connected Component (Social, 4–14 / 15–30), Diameter (DBpedia, 4–14 / 15–30), Max Clique (DBLP), MIS and MVC (Social, 4–14 / 15–30), Max Common Subgraph and GED (PubChemQC molecules, 4–9 / 10–20), TSP (OpenFlights weighted, 4–9 / 10–20, completed with shortest-distance edges). Sampling: random walk with restart, keep most-visited nodes; molecules sampled by size. Ground truth "generated by corresponding exact algorithms" / exhaustive search. Outcome categories: missing, hallucinatory (infeasible), suboptimal (feasible, not optimal), correct. Code: github.com/squareRoot3/GraphArena. — [arXiv HTML GraphArena](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00379v2); [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00379)
- GraphWiz / GraphInstruct (Nuo Chen, Yuhan Li, Jianheng Tang, Jia Li; arXiv 2402.16029, v5 Jul 2024; KDD 2024 not verified from fetched page). ### Inferences
- For a controlled study that needs exact structure and reliable labels, the safest sources are generators whose labels come from executed algorithms (GraphQA/NetworkX, Luo et al. GraphInstruct, CLRS) or exact solvers (GraphArena); GraphWiz's reasoning paths are LLM-generated and filtered only by final-answer correctness, so use its labels but not its paths as ground truth.
- Difficulty can be controlled on two axes used across generators: node count tiers (5–7 / 8–15 / 16–25 / 26–35 or 4–14 / 15–30) and edge probability; NLGraph's finding that special structures (chains, cliques) behave differently argues for stratifying by generator family and reporting per-family results.
- For text-attributed graphs with verbatim text, TAPE (Cora/PubMed/arxiv/arxiv-2023) and CS-TAG (Amazon/GoodReads/DBLP CSVs) are the releases with raw text; Platonov's roman-empire gives exact syntactic structure but only fastText features, so it suits structure experiments rather than text experiments.
- Code: POJ-104 and CodeNet give exact labels (problem ID, plus CodeNet's accept status and runtime), while the vulnerability datasets' 25–60 % label accuracy and double-digit leakage rates are documented grounds to exclude them.
